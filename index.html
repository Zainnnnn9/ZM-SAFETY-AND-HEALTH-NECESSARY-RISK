R1
Reinforcing loop

Fatigue versus productivity

  1. Targets rise, so the fastest lever is human effort: longer shifts, fewer breaks.
  2. Output goes up for a while.
  3. Fatigue builds, and with it errors, injuries and delay.
  4. Delay creates pressure to push harder.
  5. …and the loop runs again, faster.

Most of this harm never appears in an incident log. It arrives years later as stroke and heart disease, away from the site, and the organisation that set the pace rarely carries the cost.

Leverage pointSustainable work pacing: set rates and schedules around human capacity, and track hours as a leading indicator. The WHO and ILO call for caps on working time and bans on mandatory overtime.1

Evidence file

  • Long working hours led to 745,000 deaths from stroke and ischemic heart disease in 2016, a 29% increase since 2000.1
  • From 2000 to 2016, heart-disease deaths from long hours rose 42% and stroke deaths 19%.1
  • Working 55+ hours a week is associated with an estimated 35% higher risk of stroke and 17% higher risk of dying from heart disease, compared with 35–40 hours.1
  • The analysis pooled 37 studies on heart disease (768,000+ participants) and 22 on stroke (839,000+), with surveys from 154 countries.1
  • Long working hours account for about one-third of the estimated work-related burden of disease, the largest single occupational risk factor.1
R2
Reinforcing loop

Procurement pressure

  1. Procurement is rewarded for cutting cost, so the lowest bid wins.
  2. The winner recovers margin from training, supervision, equipment and time.
  3. The most hazardous tasks move down the chain to the least protected workers.
  4. The client's own safety record improves as risk leaves its books.
  5. …which rewards outsourcing more.

BLS defines a contractor as a worker employed by one firm but working at the direction of another firm that controls the site.6 The gap between who employs and who controls is where accountability slips.

Leverage pointContract structure: make safety performance and realistic schedules part of what wins a bid, and make the controlling firm share accountability for contractor outcomes.

Evidence file

  • BLS began identifying contract workers in its fatality census in 2011: 542 of 4,693 deaths, or 12%.5
  • The share rose to 15% in 2012 and about 16–17% in 2013.64
  • In 2014, 802 contract or temporary workers died on the job, 16.7% of the total.7
  • Falls to a lower level (31%) and being struck by objects or equipment (39%) caused most contract-worker deaths in 2013.6
  • In 2013, 35% of contract workers who died in construction were contracted to a different industry.4
R3
Reinforcing loop

Automation trade-offs

  1. Automation is introduced to cut cost and remove hazardous tasks.
  2. Machines raise the pace, and targets for people rise to match.
  3. New hazards appear: repetitive strain, collisions, cognitive load.
  4. They land in new injury categories, so the "safety gain" story survives.
  5. …and more automation is justified.

Robotic drive units were meant to save warehouse workers from walking miles a shift. Instead, pick-rate expectations rose about fourfold, and the injuries followed the pace.9

Leverage pointLearning budget: reinvest part of every automation gain into studying how the work actually changed, and set targets from what is found.

Evidence file

  • In 2019, Amazon sortable facilities with robots had 7.9 serious injuries per 100 workers, more than 54% higher than non-robotic sites.8
  • In 2021 the gap persisted: 7.3 vs. 5.7 per 100.10
  • Workers at robotic centres were expected to pick about 400 items an hour, against a standard of about 100.9
  • After robots arrived at the Tracy, California centre, its serious-injury rate nearly quadrupled, reaching 11.3 per 100 workers in 2018.11
B1
Balancing loop

Regulatory delay

  1. A hazard appears and harm accumulates.
  2. Evidence builds, often slowly, because of B2.
  3. Rulemaking begins, then stalls in review and challenge.
  4. A rule takes effect decades later, with thin enforcement.
  5. …by which time the system has moved on.

Regulation is the system's brake. A brake applied this rarely, using incomplete information, cannot control a system this fast.

Leverage pointPolicy fast track: interim standards for emerging hazards, deadlines on review stages, and enforcement targeted by data rather than spread thin.

Evidence file

  • Federal OSHA has only enough inspectors to visit each workplace once every 191 years: about 5 inspectors per million workers, the lowest in at least 45 years.2
  • When the first Death on the Job report was published in 1992, the figure was 84 years.2
  • NIOSH recommended halving the silica exposure limit in 1974. OSHA adopted that limit in 2016: 42 years later.13
  • A 90-day White House review of the silica rule took 921 days.14 OSHA estimated the final rule would save about 600 lives a year.15
R4
Reinforcing loop

Environmental rebound

  1. Ships, trucks and warehouses get more efficient per unit.
  2. Moving goods gets cheaper.
  3. Cheaper movement means more of it.
  4. Volume growth outruns efficiency, so total impact rises.
  5. …while reports celebrate the per-unit gain.

This is the Jevons Paradox. Intensity metrics almost always improve, so they dominate sustainability reporting. Absolute totals get much less attention.

Leverage pointGreen audit transparency: report absolute impact next to intensity, across the whole chain, and ask whether the total actually fell.

Illustrative model. Today = 100.

Evidence file

  • Total shipping GHG emissions rose from 977 to 1,076 million tonnes CO₂e between 2012 and 2018, a 9.6% increase.16
  • Over the same period, the carbon intensity of shipping operations improved by about 11%, but activity growth outstripped the gains.17
  • Shipping's share of global emissions rose from 2.76% to 2.89%.18
  • Under business-as-usual scenarios, emissions could rise by up to 50% by 2050 relative to 2018, despite further efficiency gains.19
B2
Balancing loop

Under­reporting

  1. Reporting has a personal cost: blame, lost bonuses, lost shifts.
  2. Low incident counts are rewarded.
  3. So incidents go unreported, and the numbers look good.
  4. Good numbers reduce scrutiny from leaders, clients and regulators.
  5. …and every other brake weakens.

This is the loop that disables the others. If harm isn't reported, regulation can't respond, procurement can't price it and leadership can't see it.

Leverage pointHonest, protected reporting: reward reports, protect reporters, measure reporting quality, and treat a sudden drop in numbers with curiosity rather than celebration.
68

Evidence file

  • Modelling of the US Bureau of Labor Statistics survey estimated it missed between 33% and 69% of all nonfatal injuries.20
  • In Michigan, researchers estimated 869,034 work injuries and illnesses a year (1999–2001), against a BLS estimate of 281,567.21 They attributed about 75% of missed cases to employer underreporting.22
  • For agriculture, the survey was estimated to miss 77.6% of cases.23
  • BLS's own review notes undercount estimates from 20% to 70%, and lists employee fear about reporting among the causes.24

vii.

Mapping is half the work.

Mapping the system from the outside is only half the picture. The other half is the programs, data and reporting that make these loops visible. How they are designed decides what anyone can see.

01

Data can expose blind spots or confirm them

A system that only captures what's easy to count reproduces the blind spots exactly. Designing the data model is a safety and sustainability decision.

02

Safety and sustainability are one system

Procurement pressure, automation and weak reporting drive harm to people and the planet at the same time. Separate teams with separate metrics hide the connections.

03

The leverage is in the questions

Where is risk being transferred? Which numbers look suspiciously good? Is total impact actually falling, or only intensity? Build reporting around those questions.

Look for the loop, not just the incident.

ix.

Method and limits.

Approach

  • Causal loop analysis from system dynamics: reinforcing loops (R) amplify change, balancing loops (B) resist it.
  • Each loop is matched against published evidence from international bodies, regulators, government statistics, peer-reviewed research and investigative reporting. Every figure links to its source.
  • The system harm index, rebound chart, loop audit and hidden-cost estimate are illustrative tools built on the cited figures where possible.
  • The 2035 trendlines are ZM's straight-line extrapolations of the cited data points.

Limits

  • The loops are a lens, not proof of causation. The evidence is consistent with each loop, and other explanations exist for many patterns.
  • Figures come from different countries, years and methods. Most enforcement and injury data is from the United States, where the longest public series exist.
  • Warehouse figures come from one company's internal records as analysed by journalists and a labour coalition, and the company has disputed some characterisations.
  • The places with the least data, such as informal and offshore work, are often where harm is greatest. That gap is part of loop B2.
